At the river, two paths part beneath a willow.

You stop where the roots have lifted the paving stones. Behind you, the station clock strikes an hour you cannot count. The sound arrives slowly, softened by the fields, and fades before you are certain it has finished.

There is a bench beneath the tree. Its wooden slats are silver with age, polished smooth in the place where generations of hands have rested. Someone has carved a small circle into one end. Inside the circle is a shape that might be a leaf, or a flame, or a boat seen from very far away.

You sit for a while. The night is cool enough to make you notice the warmth still held in your coat. You put your hands in your pockets and find a train ticket, a loose thread, and a key whose lock is somewhere behind you.

Across the water, the lit window brightens. A figure passes in front of it, pauses, and moves away. You cannot tell whether they have seen you. You cannot tell whether it matters.

A current turns beneath the surface. It carries a fallen leaf towards the bridge, then draws it gently into the dark beneath the bank. For several seconds you follow the place where the leaf should be, even after there is nothing left to see.

You remember being told that rivers are always going somewhere. As a child, you had imagined that this meant they knew the way. Now, listening to the water make its small adjustments around the stones, you wonder whether knowing has anything to do with it.

A breeze lifts the willow branches. They brush together with a dry, papery sound. A few touch the river and leave widening circles behind them. The circles meet, pass through one another, and disappear.

From the house comes the faint sound of a door closing. Then a cupboard, perhaps. Then silence again. These ordinary sounds make the distance between the two banks seem smaller than it looked from the road.

You stand and smooth the bench with your palm, though you have left nothing on it. The carved circle catches the lantern light. For a moment its little shape looks unmistakably like an open hand. When you look again, it is only a mark in the wood.

To your left, a lantern sways above a narrow footbridge. Someone has left the gate open.

To your right, pale stones lead down to the shore. The water is very still. Somewhere in the reeds, a bird calls once.

There is no signpost, and no sound now from the road behind you. You take the key out of your pocket, turn it once between your fingers, and put it away. Whichever path you choose, you will have to leave the bench.

For a moment, both ways seem familiar. You let that feeling settle, and then you choose.
